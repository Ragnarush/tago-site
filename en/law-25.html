Quebec's Act 25 and you

What Quebec's personal information law asks of a self-employed worker who keeps clients' contact details, and what TAGO does — or doesn't do — to help. This page summarizes publications of the Commission d'accès à l'information (CAI), Quebec's privacy regulator; it is not legal advice. The law always prevails, and a lawyer can assess your situation. It applies to businesses carried on in Quebec.

1. Does it apply to you?

Yes, as soon as you carry on a business in Quebec and hold information about people: a client's name, email, phone number or address, what you billed them. The Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector, as amended by Act 25 (Bill 64), makes no exception for one-person businesses; its obligations apply in proportion to what you hold.

2. A person in charge of the protection of personal information

By default, it's the person with the highest authority in the business — you. You can delegate the role in writing. Their title and contact details must be published on the business's website or, if you have none, made available by other appropriate means (for example, on your invoices or on request).

In TAGO: ⋮ › About › Privacy (Act 25) lets you enter the designated officer; left blank, the policy built into the app says it's the business owner. This field publishes nothing for you.

3. Your rules, published in plain terms

Since September 2023, a business must have policies and practices governing what it does with personal information — what, why, for how long, who has access, how a complaint is handled — and publish detailed information about them, in clear and simple terms, on its website or by other appropriate means. For a self-employed worker, a few paragraphs often suffice: what you collect about your clients, what for, where it's kept, for how long, and whom to write to.

If you collect information through technological means, such as a form on your website, a published privacy policy is also required.

What TAGO can give you to write it: your data is on your phone, in the app's private storage; TAGO has no server and never sees it; backups are encrypted with a passphrase only you know; nothing is sold or shared. TAGO's privacy policy lists every network exchange.

4. Confidentiality incidents

An incident is any unauthorized access to, use or communication of personal information, or its loss. A lost or stolen phone holding your client records is one. The business must then:

  • take reasonable measures to reduce the risk of injury and to prevent it from happening again;
  • notify the Commission and the people concerned if the incident presents a risk of serious injury — based on how sensitive the information is, the anticipated consequences and the likelihood of malicious use;
  • record every incident in a register, even those without serious risk, and keep it at least five years; the Commission may ask for a copy.

What limits the damage with TAGO: lock your phone (without a lock, whoever holds it opens the app); an encrypted backup lets you recover everything on a new device without the copy itself being readable by whoever finds it. TAGO doesn't keep an incident register: a simple dated document will do.

5. Your clients' requests

A client can ask you, in writing, for the information you hold about them, and have it corrected. You have 30 days to answer; no answer counts as a refusal. Since September 2024, they can also ask for the computerized information collected from them in a structured, commonly used technological format (portability).

In TAGO, most of what you hold about a client is on their record (⋮ › Clients): contact details, recent documents and Statement of account, a PDF of all their documents and payments. Each invoice can be shared as a PDF, and the Revenue list can be shared as CSV, a format any spreadsheet opens.

6. Keep, then destroy

Once the purpose for which you collected information is fulfilled, it must be destroyed or anonymized — subject to retention periods set by law. Your invoices and supporting documents fall under tax laws, which generally require keeping them six years after the end of the tax year they relate to.

In TAGO, deleting a client who has documents archives them rather than erasing invoices you must keep; a client with no documents is deleted for good.

7. What leaves Quebec

Before communicating personal information outside Quebec, a business must carry out a privacy impact assessment and make sure the information will be adequately protected there.

With TAGO, two optional features send data off your phone, and you turn them on. Sending documents by email with your Brevo key sends Brevo (France, European Union) the PDF and the client's name and email: that's a communication outside Quebec, and the assessment is yours. Backups to your Google Drive are encrypted before they leave: Google only receives an unreadable file, with its name, size and date. Sharing a PDF through your own email app goes through that email provider, like any email you send.
